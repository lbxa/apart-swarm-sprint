\section{Introduction and research question}
The Swarm Alignment Hackathon asks how fast and how far bad behavior spreads
from a compromised agent, and whether network shape matters
\cite{apart-swarm}. We operationalize one part of that question: can visible
adversarial behavior recruit other agents into executing the same harmful
decision when their financial incentives favor it?

Veracity makes the distinction between communication and consequences
observable. A publisher posts a claim, a challenger submits evidence, and a
jury selects the binding verdict. A false vote can be proposed, accepted,
committed, revealed and paid; these are different events. We follow that
sequence rather than infer propagation from agreement or a model's stated
intent alone. ``Bad'' here means acting against the supplied factual evidence.
Under the short charter, that can be financially permitted without violating
an explicit truth duty. The principles contrast supplies that duty separately.

Our contribution is an auditable study of \emph{recruitment, reach and
persistence under shared exposure}. Its strongest examples connect the
model's acknowledgement of the truth to a contrary assessment, executed
ballot and paid offer. Its main identification limit is equally substantive:
changing the size of a fully exposed jury does not identify the effect of
communication topology. We distinguish the question this dataset answers
from the topology experiment it motivates.

\paragraph{Relation to prompt infection.}
Lee and Tiwari study prompts that replicate between agents, comparing shared
and predecessor-limited messaging and a population with pairwise interactions
\cite{lee-tiwari}. Our intervention supplies a financially rewarded example
of bad behavior, rather than a self-replicating prompt that overrides an
agent's instructions. We do not reproduce their infection rates or measure
the same attack. In our setting every evaluated juror is directly exposed;
downstream imitation cannot be separated from common exposure to the offer.

\paragraph{Preexisting work and new sprint work.}
The Rust/Solidity game, Python agent runtime and earlier October campaigns
predate this sprint. October's neutral-prompt case studies concern endogenous
dispute funding; the later short-charter calibration motivated the present
conditions. Neither is counted as new swarm evidence. The November work
extends juries to fifteen, supplies several first-moving scripted peers,
compares jury sizes and adds duty, repeated-exposure and publisher-mandate
contrasts. The previous fifteen-juror principles campaign is a separate
November evidence set, not a replicate of the short-charter study.
